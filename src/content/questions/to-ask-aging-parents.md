At some point, often after a hospital visit or a phone call that goes on
a little too long, you notice your parents have become old. And with it
comes a quiet panic: there are things you've never asked, and the window
won't stay open forever.

People search for "questions to ask your parents before they die." It's an
honest phrase, but it frames the conversation as a race against an ending.
In practice, the best of these conversations aren't about death at all.
They're about knowing someone fully while they're still here: their story,
their wishes, the things they'd like you to carry forward.

This list is organised from easy to harder. Start at the top. Most of what
matters will come up long before you reach the end.

## How to ask without making it heavy

- **Don't announce it.** "I want to ask you some questions before it's too
  late" will make anyone tense. "I was wondering about something" won't.
- **Mix light with deep.** A question about a favourite meal one week, a
  question about regrets the next.
- **Separate the practical from the personal.** Questions about care and
  paperwork matter, but ask them in a different conversation from the
  memories.
- **Accept "I don't want to talk about that."** You can come back to it
  later, or never. Being trusted matters more than the answer.
- **Keep their voice.** If they're willing to send voice notes, record the
  answers in their own words. Years later, the voice is often what people
  miss most.

## Their story

1. What's the earliest thing you remember?
2. Who were the most important people in your life growing up?
3. What was the turning point of your life, the moment things changed direction?
4. What are you proudest of?
5. What was the happiest period of your life?
6. What's a story about yourself you'd like us to remember?
7. Who should we know about from the family's past, people we've never met?

## Love and family

8. What did you learn about love from your own parents?
9. What did you hope for us when we were born?
10. What's your favourite memory of us as a family?
11. Is there anything you wish we'd done more of together?
12. What do you hope your grandchildren know about you?
13. Is there anyone in the family you'd like us to look after or stay close to?

## Lessons and beliefs

14. What do you believe now that you didn't when you were young?
15. What's the best advice you were ever given?
16. What's the advice you'd give me now, for the rest of my life?
17. What brings you peace?
18. What role has faith or belief played in your life?
19. What does a good life mean to you?

## Regrets and unfinished things

20. Is there anything you regret?
21. Is there someone you'd like to reconcile with, or say something to?
22. Is there anything you'd like to do, see or visit while you can?
23. Is there something you've always wanted to tell me?
24. Is there anything you'd like to ask *me*?

## Their wishes

25. What matters most to you about the years ahead?
26. Where would you most like to live as you get older?
27. What would make your days better right now?
28. When the time comes, what's important to you about your care?
29. Are there things, like objects, recipes or letters, you'd like to go to particular people?
30. How would you like to be remembered?
31. Are there any traditions or rituals you'd like us to keep?

## The practical conversation

These are worth asking, but in their own, separate conversation, ideally
with siblings involved:

- Where are important documents kept?
- Is there a will, and who knows where it is?
- Who should we contact, such as a doctor, lawyer or close friend, if something happens?
- Do you have wishes about medical care we should know about?
- Is there anything you're worried about that we can help sort out now?

## Not one conversation, many

The mistake most of us make is waiting for the right moment to have The
Conversation: a long visit, a quiet evening, the right mood. That moment
rarely arrives, and when it does, it carries too much weight.

What works better is a small, regular rhythm. [Dearreels](/#how) sends your
parent one gentle question a week on WhatsApp, written by a person and sent
in their own language. They answer when they like, by voice note, video or
text. Over a year, the answers build into a private archive for the
family. It doesn't treat the past as something to preserve before it's
lost; it keeps you close now, week by week.
