The call usually happens on Sunday. Sometimes it's Saturday, if the week
went badly, or Monday, if you forgot. It lasts about fifteen minutes. Your
mother tells you about her knee, the neighbour's daughter's wedding, the
price of tomatoes. You tell her work is busy, the children are fine, the
weather has turned. Somebody says *achha, okay* and the call ends, and you
sit for a moment with a feeling you can't quite name.

It isn't that nothing was said. It's that nothing *new* was said. You love
her, she loves you, and the distance between you has quietly filled up with
logistics.

## The shape of the distance

Living far from your parents is not one problem. It's several, layered.

There's geography, the hours on a plane and the time zones that make
every call a negotiation. There's time, the way your life and theirs have
diverged into routines the other can't picture. And underneath both there's
something harder to say: your parents are getting older, and you're
watching it happen in fifteen-minute instalments through a phone screen.

Most of us respond to this with intensity. We plan the long visit home. We
promise ourselves a proper conversation, *the* conversation, where we'll
finally ask about her childhood, his father, the years before we were born.
Then the visit arrives, and it's full of relatives and errands and jet lag,
and the conversation doesn't happen. We fly back carrying the same
questions.

## Why big gestures don't work

There's a reason the long visit and the big conversation so often
disappoint. They carry too much weight. When a single moment has to make up
for months of distance, everyone feels it, and people rarely open up under
pressure.

Closeness, it turns out, is built from small things done often. Not the
annual holiday but the ordinary Tuesday. The friends we feel closest to are
usually not the ones we see most dramatically but the ones we hear from
regularly, in small doses, about nothing in particular, until suddenly it's
about something.

That's the quiet logic behind every family ritual that lasts: the Sunday
lunch, the evening walk, the bedtime story. They work because they're
small enough to keep doing.

## A different kind of question

If the Sunday call has flattened, the fix usually isn't a longer call. It's
a different question.

"How are you?" invites "fine." "What did your mother cook when you were sick
as a child?" invites a story. Specific questions do something general ones
can't: they give a person permission to go somewhere, and a place to start.

And they work better spread out. One good question a week, asked without
ceremony, gives a parent time to think, to remember, to answer when the
mood is right. A question sent on Monday might be answered on Thursday
morning in a voice note that starts, *I was thinking about what you asked.*
That voice note is worth more than a hundred Sunday calls about the weather.

## The guilt, honestly

Nobody who lives far from aging parents is free of guilt. You chose a life
somewhere else, for good reasons, and the cost is borne partly by people who
didn't choose it. Often it's the eldest daughter who carries it most,
the one who organises the birthdays and runs the family group chat.

Guilt is a poor engine for connection, though. It produces dutiful calls
and anxious visits, and it makes every conversation feel like a payment on a
debt. What replaces it isn't absolution; it's rhythm. When you know you'll
hear something real from your parent every week, the distance stops feeling
like a failure you have to make up for, and starts feeling like a shape your
relationship has, one you can live inside.

## Small, regular, in their language

A few things make this rhythm easier to keep:

- **Use what they already use.** For many parents, especially overseas,
  that's WhatsApp. Every new app is a small barrier; every barrier is a
  reason not to reply.
- **Let them speak.** Voice notes lower the effort of answering to almost
  nothing, and they keep something writing never does: the way a person
  actually sounds.
- **Let them use their own language.** Many parents tell their best stories
  in the language they grew up in, even if your calls are in another.
- **Don't keep score.** Some weeks there'll be a paragraph, some weeks a
  two-second reply, some weeks nothing. That's fine. The rhythm matters more
  than any single answer.
- **Share it with siblings.** If your brothers and sisters are scattered
  too, everyone hearing the same stories is its own kind of closeness.

## What you're really keeping

We built [Dearreels](/#how) around exactly this rhythm. One question a week
on WhatsApp, written by a person, in your parent's language. They answer
however and whenever they like. At the end of the year, the family receives
an archive of every reply.

But the archive isn't really the point. The point is the Tuesday morning
when your phone lights up with your father's voice, telling you about a
summer in 1971 you've never heard of. The point is feeling close now,
week by week, to someone who lives too far away.

The distance won't shrink. But the space inside it can fill with something
other than logistics.
